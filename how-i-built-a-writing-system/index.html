How I built a writing system that argues with me

I spent twenty years reading and posting and never finishing anything. This fall I built a system, with Claude, that turns my arguments into published pieces. When a detector says a piece is AI-written, it's measuring the sentences. This is an account of everything the sentences sit on top of, and most of it is me.

Vikas ErraballiAbout 15 min

The problem

I had the raw material of a writer and none of the output. Fifteen years of Kindle highlights, Evernote notes going back to 2009, a few thousand saved articles, and about 22,000 Facebook posts and comments, many of them first drafts of essays I never wrote. What I didn't have was a finishing process. And the obvious shortcut had a trap in it: readers now run a piece through a detector, get a number, and stop reading. I understand the reflex. Most of what the detectors catch is a thesis fed to a model and posted. But the number measures the sentences, and the sentences are the last and smallest part of a piece. So the goal was a system that keeps the thinking mine and makes it visible: the model does research, fact-checking and the twenty boring checks, and I do the part a detector can't see, which is deciding what's true and arguing with the draft until it is.

This is an experiment, and the system is still changing week to week. What follows is how it's put together, with the Florida Senate piece as the worked example. Florida was the first time the system had to hold up under a real argument: five drafts in a day, and every draft taught it something. One piece proves very little, though, and I'll come back to what would.

A memex, eighty years late

None of this started with AI. In July 1945 Vannevar Bush, who had run American science through the war, published "As We May Think" in The Atlantic and described a desk he called the memex: a private library on microfilm, fast enough to consult that it would be "an enlarged intimate supplement to his memory." His complaint was that libraries are indexed by alphabet and class, and minds aren't. "The human mind does not work that way. It operates by association." So the memex let its owner tie any two items together, build named trails through everything he'd read, write his own notes into the trail, and pull the whole trail up years later in the middle of a conversation. "The process of tying two items together is the important thing."

I came to the essay late. A month after the 2016 election I sent four friends a write-up for what I called a civic platform. It would sit one layer above Snopes and PolitiFact, where people you already trust do the fact-checking they'd be doing anyway and organize it for everyone else. Facts would be kept apart from views, each issue would be a page maintained like a Wikipedia entry, and from any page you could cross over to how another group saw the same issue, "to allow for cross-pollination of opposing views, synthesis when possible."

A month later, with a highlighted printout of Tim Wu's The Attention Merchants on the dining table, I sketched the reading half on a dozen notepad pages. Every article would carry five layers you could switch on and off: the article itself, the names and entities a machine could pick out of it, my highlights and annotations, an outline sorted from those highlights, and its relations to everything else I'd read. Any layer could be missing. The line I wrote at the bottom of the page was the one that mattered: "If more than 1 layer(s) present there must be a relationship between the layer(s)." I posted a photo of the pages the next day, with a caption about laying the layers out in space instead of scrolling one screen: "See the whole, not just a piece."

By late January I had a note that read "Essays should be able to talk to each other, or too much replication of effort," and another saying the tool should aim at Adler's ideal of dialectical objectivity: "To look at all sides and take no sides." In February, introducing myself to a group working on the same problem, I wrote that I'd been designing "something I saw later originally conceived of by Vannevar Bush." That fall I was trading notes on the layers with a group planning the fiftieth anniversary of Douglas Engelbart's 1968 demo, the line that runs from Bush's desk to the hyperlink.

Over the next few years I drew up schemas and built prototypes of the same thing: a library where the articles talk to each other, where my annotations sit on the page the way Hypothesis lets you mark up the web, and where citing a source while I write takes one step. Every version stalled on the same problem Bush's design had. Someone has to make each link between two items by hand, and I was never going to make ten thousand links on the chance I'd need one.

What changed is that the hand labor is gone. A model can search my highlights by idea instead of by keyword, which is the closest thing yet to Bush's selection by association, and it can connect a nine-year-old clipping to tonight's draft in the time it takes me to ask. The library this system runs on is the memex I kept sketching, with the one part Bush couldn't mechanize filled in.

There's one more note from that stretch, and I'd forgotten writing it. In February 2019, twelve days after OpenAI announced GPT-2, I was reading its answers to test prompts and wrote that if machine text flooded the environment, my filter would let in less, and that the tool worth building was one that flags the original idea in the flood. Further down the page I wrote that "anything that could be generated by AI, should be, leaving humans to do better things." That's the division of labor this account describes, set down almost four years before ChatGPT by someone with no idea how to build it. The same note predicted that sensemaking would co-evolve with efforts to confuse it. The detector and the prose it grades are one arms race, and I'm not entering it.

Source material first

The first practical decision was that a writer's references should come from what he has actually read. That sounds obvious, but the default behavior of any model is to reach for the quote everyone reaches for. So before any writing, I consolidated everything into one place: the Kindle and Audible highlights, the Goodreads and Amazon lists, the Evernote archive, the saved links from four platforms, even screenshots of book pages from my phone. From that came a quote bank, one file per book with my highlights and margin notes; a commonplace book of ideas by theme; and a sheet of every author I've engaged with, tiered by how much I trust them. The rule the system follows: a quote earns its place only if it sharpens the argument, it must come from a book I've read or own, and three references is usually the ceiling.

Three guides, three jobs

A model that knows your reading still writes in its own voice, so the second layer is three guides it reads before every draft. The voice guide was mined from my own writing: the moves that are mine (analogy as the main argument, mechanism before morality, stated odds with the reason behind them, a credential worn lightly) and the habits I wanted to lose (sarcasm as a rebuttal, contempt words, stacked rhetorical questions). The craft guide is how a piece works, built from the writing advice I had saved for fifteen years without ever applying: Palahniuk on received text, Mamet's boredom test, Roy Peter Clark on aiming a long sentence at one word. Every rule names its source. The frameworks guide is the one I'm proudest of. It's how I think: the nine analytical lenses I got from the books that changed how I see the world, each with the questions it makes me ask. The voice guide is how I sound. This one is why my pieces aren't interchangeable with anyone else's.

The voice guide has an odd origin. In October, getting ready to put my own name on my Facebook account, I went through twenty years of it: four thousand posts archived, thirteen thousand comments read one at a time. Anything strident or sarcastic went, whatever the topic. Reading all of it to decide what to hide turned out to be the best map I'll get of how I argue. The same pass that flagged the sarcasm pulled out 1,300 places where I'd explained something, answered pushback, made the other side's case or changed my mind. The guide is built from the second list. The first list became its don'ts.

Two rules from the craft guide, as examples of the kind of thing that's in there:

Craft guideNo received text; invent your own. A phrase worn smooth by other writers ("wringing my hands") that the writer never actually pictured. If something has been described one way, find a new way, in terms only this writer would use. The same goes for aphorisms: coin the line only this piece could have.
Craft guideAim long sentences at one word. A long sentence works when it flies toward an emphatic final word. Put the payoff at the end.

The workflow for one piece

I send a thesis, sometimes two lines. The model finds the one-sentence version of what I'm saying, names the lens it runs on, checks every fact, searches my reading by idea rather than keyword, runs a reversal test and writes the strongest case against, then edits rather than rewrites. Then two passes I added after the first published piece came back sounding like a machine: a second read "as Vikas would," and a scan for the tells of model prose: signpost openers, a closer on every paragraph, "not X but Y" reversals, threes for rhythm, colon reveals, abstractions as characters. Any draft that fails those doesn't reach me.

That was the system on November 1. What happened over the next three days is the part worth writing about.

The Florida piece, draft by draft

The thesis of the Florida piece was simple: Chuck Schumer's committees spent nothing on Angie Nixon's Senate race in Florida, she lost by under three points, and that was a decision rather than an oversight. Here's what each draft changed, and what the system learned.

Arguing with the draft

The thing I do that the system couldn't, at first, is read a draft the way a hostile, intelligent reader would and ask the questions that reader would ask. Are we putting words in Schumer's mouth? He never said that; Gillibrand did. Why is Ohio the comparison, when Maine, where the party spent $38 million against a senator in a state Harris carried, makes the point better? If the socialist label cost Nixon less than a point against the centrist, couldn't you say the lack of money cost her just as little? What would a $15 million buy have done to Republican spending in response? Every one of those questions made the piece better, and every one of them was a question I'd had to write by hand.

So the next move was the one that makes this a system rather than a chat: I asked for the questions to be performed before I see the draft. The craft guide now has a reader's pushback pass, fourteen questions answered and fixed on every handover, each of which came from a note I once had to make myself. The handover I get back lists what the pass changed, then only what needs me: judgment calls, personal lines, odds I have to own.

Scaling the work without losing the thread

By v4 the piece had a dozen factual threads and I started to worry about the obvious limit: one model, one context, trying to hold research, drafting and checking at once gets sloppy in exactly the way a tired writer does. The fix was division of labor. Each thread of research goes to its own agent with a clean slate and a narrow brief, and comes back as a short, sourced report. The draft is written from those reports. Then a separate agent that never saw the drafting checks every figure against the sources and reports what's wrong, problems first. The pushback pass runs on the whole draft at the end, because comparisons and framing only show up when you read the thing whole.

None of this is new. It's how a good magazine already works: reporters gather the facts, a writer drafts from their notes, a fact-checker who had no hand in the story checks every claim against the source, and an editor asks the questions a hostile reader would. Those roles exist because nobody, human or model, reliably catches their own mistakes. What's new is that I can staff every desk for a single op-ed at eleven at night. My job is the one at the top of the masthead: what the piece argues, and whether it runs.

Two desks stay human on purpose, mine and the outside reader's. A friend's hindsight objection did more for Florida than any agent, and I trust that desk for the reason the 2016 design trusted friends over websites. You can ask a friend what he's after.

What "AI-written" leaves out

The model is better than I am at four things: finding what the record actually says, holding thirty figures straight, catching my own tells, and running the same twenty checks on draft five that it ran on draft one without getting bored. It is worse than I am at the thing that matters more than the four: knowing what I think. The thesis was mine. The lens, marking to market, came from a trading floor I sat on. The reversal that rebuilt the piece came from a question I asked. The friend who said it was hindsight was mine, and so was the decision to put his objection in the piece and test it against the numbers rather than wave it off. The model typed the sentences, and I'll say so every time. But "who typed it" is a question about the last and smallest step. It tells you nothing about whether the Florida piece is right.

That's what I'd like this account to change. "AI-created" is doing a lot of work in people's heads right now: it means careless, it means generic, it means nobody checked. Those are real failures, and they're failures of process, not of tools. Here the process was five drafts, three research agents, an independent fact-check that found thirteen errors, fourteen standing questions, and a reader who wouldn't let a comparison stand if it pointed the wrong way. Judge the piece on whether the argument holds, and on whether you understand the race better for having read it. Adler, whose four grounds for disagreement already sit in the pushback pass, put one level of reading above all the others in How to Read a Book and called it syntopical: read everyone worth reading on a question, set them against each other, and come away with a view none of them hands you. That's the job the checks serve. A draft that survives them has earned the right to be argued with, and the arguing is how I find out whether I've gotten closer to true.

The part that compounds is the rule-extraction. Each time I make a correction that would apply to the next piece, it goes into a log as a before-and-after pair with the rule it teaches; the rule is stored in its general form, and the correction that produced it stays attached as the worked example. The system doesn't get smarter because the model does. It gets smarter because it keeps a record of every note I had to make by hand, so the next draft needs one fewer.

For anyone who wants to build the same thing, the parts are ordinary. The whole system is a Claude project. The guides are documents the model reads before every draft; the drafting procedure is a skill, a file of instructions; research and fact-checking run as separate agents with clean contexts; the quote bank and the corrections log are files in Drive. There is no custom software in the loop, and when a draft needs arithmetic, like the fifty-state swing table, the model writes and runs the code itself. The leverage is all in the rulebook, and in where the independence sits.

What I expect to break

Before asking how a position makes money, ask how it loses it. This system has a few obvious ways to fail, and most of them follow from how the models actually work, which is the part that usually gets skipped when people argue about whether AI can write. Knowing the mechanism tells you where to put the checks.

What's next

One piece is a single data point. The proof of the system will be in the next ten, and the measure I care about is whether each one needs fewer of my notes than the last, because that is what it looks like when the rules are capturing how I think. On the list: figures and citations in every piece, with every number checked by a fact-checking agent that had no part in writing the draft. A historical-analogues doc, because half of what I write is forecasting and the reading I do for it should be organized by the question it answers. And a prediction log, so I keep score, misses included.

The writing system is one half of those 2017 pages. The other half was for readers. My cousin, one of the four who got the 2016 write-up, read a draft of this page and went straight to it. Rap Genius put a crowd's annotations under every lyric, so put this machinery under everything else. Hover on a claim in Das Kapital and see the strongest objections and who made them. Hover on a line in a mayor's plan for city-run grocery stores and see how the idea has fared in the cities that tried one. That's the five layers with the staffing problem solved. Crowd annotation never scaled past lyrics because it needed Bush's "new profession of trail blazers," and nobody could pay for one. A model can do that job, and the pushback pass I run on my own drafts is the same pass a reader would want run on anything he reads. For now I'm pointing it at my own backlog.

And one open position to mark honestly, since the conceit of this site demands it: I can't yet show the system saves me time. Florida ate most of a day in notes, rebuttals and checks. The bet is that the rules compound and my notes thin out, so the tenth piece costs a fraction of the first. If the tenth piece still takes a day of my pushback, then this is a very elaborate way of writing alone, and I'll say so.
