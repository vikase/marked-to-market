How I built a writing system that argues with me

I spent twenty years reading and posting and never finishing anything. This fall I built a system, with Claude, that turns my arguments into published pieces. When a detector says a piece is AI-written, it's measuring the sentences. This is an account of everything the sentences sit on top of, and most of it is me.

Vikas ErraballiAbout 13 min

The problem

I had the raw material of a writer and none of the output. Fifteen years of Kindle highlights, Evernote notes going back to 2009, a few thousand saved articles, and about 22,000 Facebook posts and comments, many of them first drafts of essays I never wrote. What I didn't have was a finishing process. And the obvious shortcut had a trap in it: readers now run a piece through a detector, get a number, and stop reading. I understand the reflex. Most of what the detectors catch is a thesis fed to a model and posted. But the number measures the sentences, and the sentences are the last and smallest part of a piece. So the goal was a system that keeps the thinking mine and makes it visible: the model does research, fact-checking and the twenty boring checks, and I do the part a detector can't see, which is deciding what's true and arguing with the draft until it is.

This is an experiment, and the system is still changing week to week. What follows is how it's put together, with the Florida Senate piece as the worked example. Florida was the first time the system had to hold up under a real argument: five drafts in a day, and every draft taught it something. One piece proves very little, though, and I'll come back to what would.

A memex, eighty years late

None of this started with AI. In July 1945 Vannevar Bush, who had run American science through the war, published "As We May Think" in The Atlantic and described a desk he called the memex: a private library on microfilm, fast enough to consult that it would be "an enlarged intimate supplement to his memory." His complaint was that libraries are indexed by alphabet and class, and minds aren't. "The human mind does not work that way. It operates by association." So the memex let its owner tie any two items together, build named trails through everything he'd read, write his own notes into the trail, and pull the whole trail up years later in the middle of a conversation. "The process of tying two items together is the important thing."

That essay has had a hold on me for a long time. I clipped it into Evernote in January 2017, and in 2020 I was copying Bush's definition into a notebook beside a sketch of Ted Nelson's parallel documents and visible links, the version of hypertext Nelson built on Bush's idea. For years I've drawn up schemas and built prototypes of the same thing: a library of everything I've read where the articles talk to each other, where my annotations sit on the page the way Hypothesis lets you mark up the web, and where citing a source while I write takes one step. Each version ran into the problem Bush's desk would have had. Somebody has to lay every trail by hand, and a trail is only worth something if somebody walks it.

What changed is the walking. A model can search my highlights by idea instead of by keyword, which is the closest thing yet to selection by association, and it can follow a trail from a 2017 clipping to a 2026 draft in the time it takes me to ask. The library this system runs on is the memex I kept sketching. The trails in it are still mine.

Source material first

The first practical decision was that a writer's references should come from what he has actually read. That sounds obvious, but the default behavior of any model is to reach for the quote everyone reaches for. So before any writing, I consolidated everything into one place: the Kindle and Audible highlights, the Goodreads and Amazon lists, the Evernote archive, the saved links from four platforms, even screenshots of book pages from my phone. From that came a quote bank, one file per book with my highlights and margin notes; a commonplace book of ideas by theme; and a sheet of every author I've engaged with, tiered by how much I trust them. The rule the system follows: a quote earns its place only if it sharpens the argument, it must come from a book I've read or own, and three references is usually the ceiling.

Three guides, three jobs

A model that knows your reading still writes in its own voice, so the second layer is three guides it reads before every draft. The voice guide was mined from my own posts: the moves that are mine (analogy as the main argument, mechanism before morality, stated odds with the reason behind them, a credential worn lightly) and the habits I wanted to lose (sarcasm as a rebuttal, contempt words, stacked rhetorical questions). The craft guide is how a piece works, built from the writing advice I had saved for fifteen years without ever applying: Palahniuk on received text, Mamet's boredom test, Roy Peter Clark on aiming a long sentence at one word. Every rule names its source. The frameworks guide is the one I'm proudest of. It's how I think: the nine analytical lenses I got from the books that changed how I see the world, each with the questions it makes me ask. The voice guide is how I sound. This one is why my pieces aren't interchangeable with anyone else's.

Two rules from the craft guide, as examples of the kind of thing that's in there:

Craft guideNo received text; invent your own. A phrase worn smooth by other writers ("wringing my hands") that the writer never actually pictured. If something has been described one way, find a new way, in terms only this writer would use. The same goes for aphorisms: coin the line only this piece could have.
Craft guideAim long sentences at one word. A long sentence works when it flies toward an emphatic final word. Put the payoff at the end.

The workflow for one piece

I send a thesis, sometimes two lines. The model finds the one-sentence version of what I'm saying, names the lens it runs on, checks every fact, searches my reading by idea rather than keyword, runs a reversal test and writes the strongest case against, then edits rather than rewrites. Then two passes I added after the first published piece came back sounding like a machine: a second read "as Vikas would," and a scan for the tells of model prose: signpost openers, a closer on every paragraph, "not X but Y" reversals, threes for rhythm, colon reveals, abstractions as characters. Any draft that fails those doesn't reach me.

That was the system on November 1. What happened over the next three days is the part worth writing about.

The Florida piece, draft by draft

The thesis of the Florida piece was simple: Chuck Schumer's committees spent nothing on Angie Nixon's Senate race in Florida, she lost by under three points, and that was a decision rather than an oversight. Here's what each draft changed, and what the system learned.

Arguing with the draft

The thing I do that the system couldn't, at first, is read a draft the way a hostile, intelligent reader would and ask the questions that reader would ask. Are we putting words in Schumer's mouth? He never said that; Gillibrand did. Why is Ohio the comparison, when Maine, where the party spent $38 million against a senator in a state Harris carried, makes the point better? If the socialist label cost Nixon less than a point against the centrist, couldn't you say the lack of money cost her just as little? What would a $15 million buy have done to Republican spending in response? Every one of those questions made the piece better, and every one of them was a question I'd had to write by hand.

So the next move was the one that makes this a system rather than a chat: I asked for the questions to be performed before I see the draft. The craft guide now has a reader's pushback pass, fourteen questions answered and fixed on every handover, each of which came from a note I once had to make myself. The handover I get back lists what the pass changed, then only what needs me: judgment calls, personal lines, odds I have to own.

Scaling the work without losing the thread

By v4 the piece had a dozen factual threads and I started to worry about the obvious limit: one model, one context, trying to hold research, drafting and checking at once gets sloppy in exactly the way a tired writer does. The fix was division of labor. Each thread of research goes to its own agent with a clean slate and a narrow brief, and comes back as a short, sourced report. The draft is written from those reports. Then a separate agent that never saw the drafting checks every figure against the sources and reports what's wrong, problems first. The pushback pass runs on the whole draft at the end, because comparisons and framing only show up when you read the thing whole.

None of this is new. It's how a good magazine already works: reporters gather the facts, a writer drafts from their notes, a fact-checker who had no hand in the story checks every claim against the source, and an editor asks the questions a hostile reader would. Those roles exist because nobody, human or model, reliably catches their own mistakes. What's new is that I can staff every desk for a single op-ed at eleven at night. My job is the one at the top of the masthead: what the piece argues, and whether it runs.

What "AI-written" leaves out

The model is better than I am at four things: finding what the record actually says, holding thirty figures straight, catching my own tells, and running the same twenty checks on draft five that it ran on draft one without getting bored. It is worse than I am at the thing that matters more than the four: knowing what I think. The thesis was mine. The lens, marking to market, came from a trading floor I sat on. The reversal that rebuilt the piece came from a question I asked. The friend who said it was hindsight was mine, and so was the decision to put his objection in the piece and test it against the numbers rather than wave it off. The model typed the sentences, and I'll say so every time. But if you read the Florida piece and the only question you have is who typed it, you've asked the one question the piece can't fail.

That's what I'd like this account to change. "AI-created" is doing a lot of work in people's heads right now: it means careless, it means generic, it means nobody checked. Those are real failures, and they're failures of process, not of tools. Here the process was five drafts, three research agents, an independent fact-check that found thirteen errors, fourteen standing questions, and a reader who wouldn't let a comparison stand if it pointed the wrong way. Judge the piece on whether the argument holds. That's the test I built the system to pass.

The rule-extraction is the part I'd tell anyone to copy. Each time I make a correction that would apply to the next piece, it goes into a log as a before-and-after pair with the rule it teaches, and the rule goes into the guide. The system doesn't get smarter because the model does. It gets smarter because it keeps a record of every time I was right and it wasn't.

What I expect to break

Years on risk desks left me with one habit I trust: before asking how a position makes money, ask how it loses it. This system has a few obvious ways to fail, and most of them follow from how the models actually work, which is the part that usually gets skipped when people argue about whether AI can write. Knowing the mechanism tells you where to put the checks.

What's next

One piece is a single data point. The proof of the system will be in the next ten, and the measure I care about is whether each one needs fewer of my notes than the last, because that is what it looks like when the rules are capturing how I think. On the list: figures and citations in every piece, with every number checked by a fact-checking agent that had no part in writing the draft. A historical-analogues doc, because half of what I write is forecasting and the reading I do for it should be organized by the question it answers. And a prediction log, so I keep score. I called Michigan right last week. I'd like to be able to show the misses too.
